Product upload sounds like data entry, so people expect a flat, obvious price. In practice quotes for the same catalogue can differ several times over, because the word "upload" covers anything from pasting a clean CSV into Shopify to rebuilding a supplier spreadsheet, writing the copy and fixing the images. This guide explains how upload is priced, what moves the number, and how to work out whether doing it in-house or outsourcing is cheaper for you.

## The four pricing models

Providers charge in one of four ways, and each hides a different risk.

**Per product.** A fixed amount per listing. It is easy to compare and forecast, but the definition of a "product" matters. Check whether variants, images and written descriptions are inside the price or billed on top.

**Per hour.** Common with freelancers and agencies. You carry the risk of slow work and rework, and the total is hard to predict before the job starts. It suits small, uncertain tasks, not a 2,000-row catalogue.

**Per project.** One fixed price for a defined scope, usually after the provider has looked at your files. This is the model we use: a fixed quote after we inspect the data, before work starts. It works best when the scope is written down.

**Monthly.** A retainer for ongoing additions, price changes and clean-up. It makes sense if you add products every week, for instance a retailer with seasonal drops or a distributor with constant supplier updates.

## What actually drives the cost

Five things do most of the work in any quote.

### Variants

Variant rows multiply the work. A t-shirt in five sizes and six colours is thirty rows, each with its own SKU, price, stock level and, ideally, a matching image. A catalogue of 500 products can easily be 4,000 rows.

### Images

Are images already hosted at public URLs, named sensibly and sized correctly? Or are they in a shared drive, in mixed formats, with the wrong dimensions? Renaming, resizing, ordering and writing alt text takes real time, and it is the step most often left out of cheap quotes.

### Description writing

Pasting supplier text is cheap. Writing original descriptions, formatting specifications and creating SEO titles is a separate job. If you want unique copy, expect it to be a significant part of the price.

### Data mess

This is the largest hidden cost. Typical supplier files have inconsistent category names, sizes written four ways ("XL", "Extra Large", "xl", "X-Large"), prices with currency symbols, duplicate SKUs and merged cells. Cleaning is slower than uploading. Most supplier files we receive need this before anything can be imported.

### Platform

Shopify imports are generally straightforward through CSV. WooCommerce variable products, Magento configurables and BigCommerce option sets each have their own structure and their own ways to go wrong, which affects the time and the skill needed.

## Typical ranges by provider type

These are rough ranges based on how the market tends to work, not published statistics. Rates vary by country, platform and what is included, so treat them as a way to sanity-check a quote.

| Provider | Typical pricing | Strengths | Weak spots |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freelancer (marketplace) | Per product or per hour; the lowest cash price | Cheap for small, clean jobs | Quality varies, no QA process, availability risk |
| Offshore BPO / data-entry firm | Per product or per hour, with minimums | Capacity for large volumes | Manual entry errors, light review, copy quality |
| Ecommerce agency | Per project or hourly, usually the highest | Strategy, design and development together | Expensive for pure upload work |
| In-house staff | Salary or hourly wage, no invoice | Full control, product knowledge | Slow, pulls people from higher-value work |
| Specialist catalogue service | Per project or monthly | Process built for volume and QA | Needs a clear scope up front |

The reasoning matters more than the numbers. Freelancers are cheapest per hour but you absorb the checking. Agencies charge for senior time that upload work rarely needs. A specialist service sits between them if the process is productised.

## A worked example: 1,000 products

Take 1,000 products, an average of three variants each, supplier data that needs moderate clean-up, and descriptions that need writing. These are illustrative assumptions, so replace them with your own.

**In-house.** Suppose a team member spends about 12 minutes per product on cleaning data, entering or formatting it, writing a description, adding images and checking. That is 200 hours, around five working weeks of a full-time person. At a loaded cost of, say, $15 to $25 an hour, the cash cost is $3,000 to $5,000, before rework and before counting what that person did not do instead. Many in-house projects also slip because daily work interrupts them.

**Outsourced.** A specialist provider uses tooling to do the repetitive work and a person to review it, so elapsed time and cost drop together. Our published starting price for a Catalog Launch (up to 1,000 products) is $399, in the table below. Your real quote depends on variants, image needs and data quality, which is why we quote after inspecting files.

The in-house option can still win if you have idle capacity and want your team to learn the catalogue. For most stores, the difference in calendar time matters as much as money, since products not live yet earn nothing.

## How to compare quotes properly

Ask every provider the same six questions, and write the answers down.

1. Are variants counted as separate products?
2. Are images downloaded, resized, renamed and given alt text, or only linked?
3. Is description writing included, and is it original?
4. Who reviews the output, and do I approve before it goes live?
5. What happens if my data is worse than the sample I sent?
6. What is the turnaround, and what does it depend on?

For reference, our turnaround is from 48 hours for eligible projects; turnaround depends on scope, catalog complexity and the quality of the data and access we receive. For a broader view of the options, see [outsourcing vs in-house vs freelancers](/guides/outsource-product-upload).

## When to do it yourself and when to hand it off

Do it yourself if you have under roughly 100 products, your supplier data is already tidy and you are comfortable with a CSV. Shopify's own import tool is fine for that, and the [Shopify bulk upload guide](/guides/shopify-bulk-product-upload) walks through it.

Hand it off when you have volume, messy files, a launch date or a migration. That is where hours disappear and mistakes get expensive.
