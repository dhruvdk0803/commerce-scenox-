Product upload gets outsourced for a simple reason: it is long, repetitive and unforgiving, and nobody on a small team wants to spend three weeks on it. But "outsourcing" covers very different options, from a freelancer on Upwork to a managed service, and tools like Matrixify sit in a separate category altogether. Here is how the five routes compare and what to check whichever one you choose.

## The five routes compared

| Route | Best for | Cost shape | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-house staff | Small catalogues, product-knowledge-heavy items | Wages, no invoice | Slow; competes with daily work |
| Freelancer (Fiverr, Upwork) | Small to mid jobs with a clear spec | Per product or hourly | Uneven quality, no backup, no QA |
| Offshore data-entry firm | Large volumes of simple data | Per product or per hour | Manual errors, light review |
| App (Matrixify, native CSV) | Clean data and someone technical | Subscription or free | You still prepare and check the file |
| Managed catalogue service | Volume, messy data, deadlines | Per project or monthly | Needs a clear scope; check the review process |

None of these is universally best. The right choice depends on how clean your data is, how many products you have and how much of your own time you can give.

## In-house

Your own team knows the products and can fix gaps on the spot. The costs are time and focus: a person doing upload is not doing merchandising, customer service or marketing. In-house works well under a few hundred products. Above that, projects tend to stretch because day-to-day work takes priority. For a calculation, see [how much product upload costs](/guides/product-upload-cost).

## Freelancers on Fiverr or Upwork

You get a low price and fast starts, and the best freelancers are excellent. The risk is variance. The person may be skilled at one platform and weak on another, there is no second pair of eyes, and if they disappear mid-project you are left with half a catalogue. The checking falls on you. If you go this way, run a paid test of 10 products first and judge the result on the live storefront.

## Apps: Matrixify and native CSV

Shopify's built-in importer is free and fine for products and variants, within its documented limits. Matrixify widens what you can import and update (collections, metafields, redirects, customers and more) and gives you more control over matching and updating existing records. Both are tools, not people. They do nothing about the real problem, which is that the data in the file is inconsistent. If your file is already clean and you are comfortable with spreadsheets, an app is the cheapest route. Our [Shopify bulk upload guide](/guides/shopify-bulk-product-upload) covers the native method.

## Offshore data-entry firms and managed services

These offer capacity. A data-entry firm typically does manual keying at scale, and the quality depends on how closely the work is reviewed. A managed catalogue service combines tooling with a review step. In our case an AI engine does the volume and a person reviews every output, and the client approves before anything goes live. Whichever you choose, ask who checks the work, because that answer matters more than the rate.

## What to ask a provider before you sign

1. Which platforms have you uploaded to recently, and can I see a result?
2. What exactly is included: images, alt text, descriptions, SEO titles, variants, collections?
3. How is the price calculated, and what could change it?
4. Who reviews the output before it reaches my store?
5. How do you access my store? (A removable staff or collaborator account is right; an owner password is not.)
6. What happens to my data, and is it used to train third-party models?
7. What happens if you find errors after approval?
8. What is the turnaround, and what does it depend on?

A provider who answers these plainly is usually worth talking to. Vagueness on items 4 and 5 is a reason to walk away.

## How to brief a provider

A good brief removes most rework. Include:

- The source files, and where the images live.
- The target platform and any apps in use.
- One finished example product exactly as you want it, including title format, description structure and tags.
- Rules for edge cases: what to do when a size or price is missing, how to name colours, whether to merge duplicates.
- Which categories or collections to use, and whether products should go live or stay as drafts.
- The deadline, and any reason it is fixed (a sale, a launch, a migration).

The finished example is the most valuable item. People follow examples far more reliably than written rules.

## QA checks before approving

Do not approve on the spreadsheet alone. Check the live or draft product pages.

1. Count: the number of products and variants in the store matches the source.
2. Spot check 10 to 20 products across categories, with extra attention to the most complex ones.
3. Variants: option names and values are right, every variant has a price and SKU, and no variant is missing.
4. Prices and compare-at prices against the source, including a few that carry sale prices.
5. Images: correct image on the correct product and variant, correct order, nothing broken, alt text present.
6. Descriptions: no leftover supplier names, HTML clutter or placeholder text.
7. SEO fields: titles and descriptions exist and are not truncated oddly.
8. Collections and tags: products landed where they should.
9. Status: products are draft or active as agreed. Nothing is live by accident.

Keep a copy of the source and the final file. If something is wrong later you will want to compare.

## When to do it yourself and when to hand it off

If you have a small clean catalogue and a few spare days, upload it yourself with native CSV. It costs nothing and you learn your own data.
